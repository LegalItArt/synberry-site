Key summary of each chapter

Part 1 Knowing the Strawberry

Ch. 1 The Strawberry as a Crop — Industry Status and Opportunity

Over the past 30 years the strawberry has been one of the fastest-improving crops in Korean protected horticulture. Yield per 10 a nearly doubled, from 1,584 kg in 1990 (Source: Guide; original data KOSIS) to 2,908 kg in 2021 (Table 1-1). Exports grew from about USD 4.86 million in 1995 to USD 53.75 million in 2020, a third of all protected-horticulture exports (Source: Protected Horticulture Guide). In 2025 they reached a record USD 72 million (Source: Ministry of Agriculture, Food and Rural Affairs [MAFRA], 2026-01-12). Yet in 2022 only 280 of 15,236 strawberry farms, or 1.84%, had adopted smart-farm technology (by number of farms; for the difference from area-based counts see 1.9). There is a wide gap between the history of productivity and the present state of technology adoption. That gap is the opportunity for a new grower.

Ch. 2 Botanical Characteristics — Physiology Centred on the Crown

The cultivated strawberry is a perennial herb of the rose family. Its stem barely elongates; instead leaves, roots, runners and trusses all arise together from a short stem called the crown. Every strawberry-growing technique therefore comes down to how to make the crown thick and well filled. This chapter puts the crown at the centre and examines in turn the structure and temperature responses of roots, leaves, runners and trusses. The physiological understanding built here becomes the yardstick for every later chapter (propagation, environmental control, nutrient solution).

Ch. 3 Varieties and Cropping Types — June-bearing and Everbearing, Forcing and Semi-forcing

Variety and cropping type are not chosen separately; they are decided as one. When flower buds form (June-bearing or everbearing) decides the harvest period, and where you want the harvest period (forcing or semi-forcing) sets the range of varieties to choose from. This chapter first sets up the two axes of classification, compares the character of Korea's main varieties, and then presents a decision framework that runs "market → cropping type → variety".

Part 2 Propagation — Half the Battle

Ch. 4 Propagation and Mother-Plant Management

The saying that "strawberry farming is transplant farming" is no exaggeration. Half the quality of a daughter plant is decided not in the summer when it is raised, but in the mother plant that sends it out. This chapter compares the four ways of propagating strawberries and sets out the practical sequence from securing disease-free mother plants to collecting daughter plants. There is one principle: without healthy mother plants there are no healthy daughter plants, and without healthy daughter plants there is no crop that year.

← 한국어판 미리보기 (Korean pages)

Ch. 5 Nursery Environment and Transplant Quality

Propagation has a single goal: "a transplant with a thick crown and sturdy roots". A good transplant is not one with lush leaves but one with a thick crown, and the difference is decided by how temperature, water and nitrogen are managed over 90 days of summer. That is the whole of this chapter.

← 한국어판 미리보기 (Korean pages)

Ch. 6 Flower Bud Initiation — The Window That Sets Yield

The ceiling on a year's strawberry yield is set in a short window, from late August to mid-September, when flower buds are formed. Once that window has passed, no master grower can raise the ceiling. Initiation has three conditions — low temperature, short days and low tissue nitrogen — and the grower's job is to bring all three together at the same time.

← 한국어판 미리보기 (Korean pages)

Ch. 7 Nursery Hygiene — Disease-free Stock, Quarantine and Anthracnose Exclusion

The front line of defence against strawberry disease is not spraying but "what comes onto the farm". The most destructive diseases arrive on plants and on people's hands rather than through the air. Nursery hygiene should therefore be not one item of crop protection but a design principle running through the whole process of propagation.

Part 3 Production in the Main Field

Ch. 8 Transplanting and Establishment

Transplanting means planting "a transplant whose initiation has been confirmed, into a prepared main field, at the right depth and in the right direction". If any one of these three conditions goes wrong, that year's first truss is shaken. This chapter covers forcing culture, from preparing the main field (soil disinfection and base fertiliser) through transplanting specifications to establishment management in the two to three weeks after transplanting. About 90% of Korean strawberry growing is forcing culture (Source: Guide), so this Part on main-field production is written for forcing as the default and notes only the differences for semi-forcing.

← 한국어판 미리보기 (Korean pages)

Ch. 9 Designing an Elevated Hydroponic System

Elevated hydroponics lets you work standing upright and avoids replant problems and soil-borne disease, but because the initial investment is large, it is a system that must pay itself back through a longer harvest period and higher yield. More than 96% of Korean hydroponics is solid-substrate culture (hydroponics in a solid growing medium), and half of that is coir. This chapter takes the four elements — bed, substrate, feeding equipment and source water — in design order, and also introduces the framework of the closed-loop (drainage-reusing) system that will become the standard.

← 한국어판 미리보기 (Korean pages)

Ch. 10 The Fundamentals of Nutrient Solution Management

Managing nutrient solution is not "hanging a standard recipe on the wall". It is a cycle: start from the source water, move the concentration with the growth stage, and check the result in the drain. This chapter covers, on a hydroponic basis, the lineage of recipes, the use of nitrogen forms, EC and feed volume by growth stage, and drain diagnosis, and it adds the standard tables for fertigation in soil.

← 한국어판 미리보기 (Korean pages)

Ch. 11 Environmental Control — Targets by Growth Stage

The first confusion beginners meet in environmental control is that "every source gives a different temperature". The cause is simple: the physiological optimum (the temperature at which the plant grows best) and the management set point (the target entered in the controller for day–night temperature management) are numbers on different levels. This chapter separates the two levels, then sets out the main items of environmental control with the emphasis on set points: temperature tables by growth stage, the measured 24-hour curve of high-yielding farms, root-zone temperature, humidity, carbon dioxide and day-length extension lighting.

← 한국어판 미리보기 (Korean pages)

Ch. 12 Flowering, Pollination and Fruit Set

The marketable-fruit rate is won or lost not when the fruit swells but when the flower opens. Uneven pollination brings malformed fruit; too heavy a fruit load brings collapse of vigour and a gap in harvest. This chapter covers running honeybees, preventing malformed fruit, standards for flower and fruit thinning, and continuous flower bud emergence management that keeps trusses coming without a break all winter.

← 한국어판 미리보기 (Korean pages)

Ch. 13 Running the Winter Crop — Insulation, Heating and Energy

Running the winter crop means solving two equations at once. One is dormancy — a question of physiology, setting the start of insulation to the variety's chilling requirement. The other is energy — a question of management, weighing the fuel cost attached to each 1 °C of night temperature against the yield gained. This chapter covers the decision to start insulation, stepwise lowering of temperature, the economics of night temperature, energy-saving technology, working rules for the severe-cold season, and the transition to spring.

Part 4 Protecting the Crop — Diseases, Pests, Disorders and Postharvest

Ch. 14 Diseases — A System for Diagnosis and Control

Depending on the source, 25–35 strawberry diseases have been reported in Korea (Source: Pest Handbook; Guide), but in practice the season is won or lost on five or six of them — grey mould, powdery mildew, anthracnose, Fusarium wilt, bud blight (Rhizoctonia) and Phytophthora crown rot. Strawberry is grown as one crop a year, so a disease that wrecks the season costs the whole year; it is a crop that can be lost "in one blow". That is why the order of control is always the same: accurate diagnosis → environmental (cultural) control → chemicals used mainly for prevention, rotating chemical groups.

← 한국어판 미리보기 (Korean pages)

Ch. 15 Insect and Mite Pests — IPM with Natural Enemies

The main battlefield in the war on strawberry pests is the underside of the leaf — chemicals and natural enemies alike must reach it. And the first principle of natural enemies is timing: one Phytoseiulus persimilis predatory mite catches about eight two-spotted spider mites a day (one measurement puts it at 30.5 eggs a day — 15.12), so releasing after an outbreak is pointless, and only preventive release before or at the very start of pest appearance works (field training know-how; Source: Chungnam Biocontrol). This chapter covers the ecological figures for the four main pests (two-spotted spider mite, aphids, thrips and fungus gnats), the use of natural enemies, and the rules for making chemicals and natural enemies coexist.

← 한국어판 미리보기 (Korean pages)

Ch. 16 Physiological Disorders — Ailments That Are Not Diseases

Disorders that arise without a pathogen are a mirror of the environment and of nutrition. One reading rule solves half of them: if symptoms start on new leaves, suspect a nutrient of low mobility (calcium, iron); if they start on old leaves, suspect a highly mobile nutrient (nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, magnesium) or a problem of excess (Source: Guide). This chapter covers tipburn, malformed fruit, fruit disorders, element-by-element deficiency diagnosis, and salts, dwarfing and plant exhaustion.

← 한국어판 미리보기 (Korean pages)

Ch. 17 Harvest and Postharvest — The Battle Against Fruit Temperature

Strawberry quality peaks at the moment of harvest; everything after is a fight to slow the decline. The variable that sets the speed is fruit temperature — the temperature of the fruit's own body. Firmness drops steeply as colour develops, and respiration burns sugar in proportion to temperature, so "how unripe to pick" and "how fast to cool" are the whole of this chapter (Source: Postharvest Manual; Guide).

← 한국어판 목차 (Korean contents)

Ch. 18 Distribution and Sales — Keeping Control of Price

"Do not start the business if you have nowhere to sell what you grow" (field training know-how) — that is why the last chapter of a cultivation textbook is about distribution. Strawberry is a large market: with production worth KRW 1.4717 trillion it is the largest of the fruit vegetables, at 25.1% of the total (Source: KREI Outlook). Yet the profit and loss of a farm that leaves its price to the auction market and one that has its own direct channels can differ two- to threefold. This chapter covers the market's numbers, the ladder of sales channels, the economics of farm-experience operations, and exports.

← 한국어판 미리보기 (Korean pages)

Part 5 Connecting — Smart Farms, Data and Management

Ch. 19 Building a Smart Farm — What Is Decided Before You Build

A smart farm is defined not by its size but by whether it "measures and controls the environment automatically" — even a single-span plastic tunnel house is a smart farm once its sensors and actuators are connected (field training know-how). And the success or failure of a facility is mostly settled before ground is broken: orientation, foundations, checking the quotation, communications and power infrastructure, and the money to replace the cladding. These are decisions that cannot be undone once the house is built, so this chapter is written in the order of "what to decide first" rather than "what to buy".

← 한국어판 미리보기 (Korean pages)

Ch. 20 Data and AI — From Records to Decisions

An integrated environmental controller only carries out the values entered; it does not judge the crop — judgement is the job of the person who reads the data (field training know-how). And the first fact the data tell is a big one: with the same Seolhyang, in the same multi-span smart farms, the top 20% of farms picked 1.95 times as much as ordinary farms, and 2.3 times as much in winter, when prices are high (Source: Forum 2019). This chapter covers the variables that made that gap, the standard for growth surveys, cases in which data changed decisions, and where AI stands today.

← 한국어판 미리보기 (Korean pages)

Ch. 21 Management and the Sixth Industry — Designing a Profitable Farm

"If you sweat and nothing is left over, it is not farming" (field training know-how). Strawberry management is won or lost in three places: scale (how many pyeong, run by how many people), timing (how much you pick in December–February, when prices are highest) and channel (who sets the price). This chapter covers how to simulate income and expenditure, scale and labour, finance and policy, and expansion into the sixth industry.
